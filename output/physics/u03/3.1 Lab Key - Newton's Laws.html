FAILURE POINTS

  • String not level. The string is kept level so the pull stays along the sensor axis and stays steady. Raise or lower the pulley (Setup step 6).
  • Hanging mass swinging. A swinging hanger makes the force reading wobble. Let it hang still before releasing the cart.
  • Not zeroing with nothing pulling. Zero with the hanger lifted, before every run. In Part C, zero both sensors that way. A leftover offset shifts the Part B intercept and the Part C comparison.
  • The string must go slack cleanly in Part A. If the string is too long, the cart reaches the pulley before the mass lands and there is no flat part. If the mass bounces off a hard floor, the string snaps tight again and puts a spike in the flat part. That’s what the towel and the Setup step 5 check are for.
  • Fit and average regions. Part A fits only the flat part. Part B averages only the steady speeding-up stretch, leaving out the release spike and everything after the slack.
  • Catching late. With 80 g the cart reaches about 1.5 m/s on 0.6 m of travel. Someone’s hand stays near the pulley.
  • Part C sign. Both sensors read positive, because each shows a pull on its own hook as positive. The two pulls point in opposite directions because of the third law: the GDX-FOR pulls the cart toward the pulley, and the cart pulls the GDX-FOR away from it. Students compare sizes, with no sign flipping.

Backups for Part C if the GDX-FOR droops

(a) Hand pull, no pulley. A student holds the GDX-FOR by hand and pulls the cart along the track by it. The two readings should still match, held or speeding up. Works for every group at once. (b) Let it slide. Let the GDX-FOR slide on the track with 100 g on the string, with the two hooks at the same height. Matt to test whether 100 g moves it smoothly. (c) Ride it on a spare cart. There are only 3 working spares (10 carts, 2 broken, 5 groups), so this can’t cover every group at once. (d) GDX-FOR in the vertical string, between the string and the hanger. The cart sensor reads more than the GDX-FOR, by about the GDX-FOR’s weight. The string passes the pull along, but this is not a 3rd-law pair, so the Part C wording on the handout would need rewriting.

STRING-LENGTH ESTIMATE — CHECK

The string is a horizontal piece (hook to pulley) plus a hanging piece (pulley to hanger). The hanger lands when the hanging piece is about the pulley height H. Whatever is left is the horizontal piece, which is how far the cart still is from the pulley. So for 40 cm of coasting room, L ≈ H + 0.40 m, wherever the cart starts. Example: H = 0.95 m gives L = 1.35 m. Starting 1.00 m out, the hanger starts 0.35 m below the pulley and falls 0.60 m. The cart speeds up for 0.60 m and then has 0.40 m to coast. The hanger’s own height only adds coasting room, so the check in Setup step 5 says “at least 40 cm.” A tempting version, L ≈ d + H − 0.40 m, is wrong: it makes the mass fall only 40 cm and leaves the cart d − 0.40 m from the pulley.

PRE-LAB KEY

  1. Newton’s three laws. (1) An object stays at rest or keeps moving at a constant velocity unless a net force acts on it. (2) ∑F = ma: net force causes acceleration; more force, more acceleration; more mass, less acceleration. (3) When A exerts a force on B, B exerts a force of the same size in the opposite direction on A. Full credit for each law in the student’s own words with its key idea.
  2. String length with H = 0.95 m. 0.95 m + 0.40 m = 1.35 m, loop to loop.
  3. Predicted v–t graph. A straight line rising from zero (speeding up at a steady rate), then a nearly flat line starting where the string goes slack, sloping down very slightly, then a sharp drop at the catch. Credit needs the rise, the flat part and the slack label.

POST-LAB KEY — FROM THE SAMPLE DATA

  1. First law, Part A. Once the mass landed, the string went slack and nothing pulled the cart forward, so the net force was about zero. Also accept “small and backward (friction), about 0.03 N.” The velocity stayed nearly constant (0.81 to 0.78 m/s), as the first law says. It was not perfectly flat: the slope was about −0.10 m/s² because rolling friction still acted backward. No credit for “the force from the string kept it going.” That is the misconception this part exists to fix.
  2. Second law, Part B. As F went up, a went up: the points rose in a straight line. With the bar on, the same F gave a smaller a, so the line was steeper. Its slope, the mass, went from 0.292 to 0.744 kg.
  3. Percent error. 3.5% (cart only) and 4.5% (cart + bar). Work is above.
  4. Error source and direction. Needs a specific cause and the direction it pushed the mass. Good answers: the averaged stretch ran into the release spike or the slack part, pulling points off the line; friction that grows with speed, so faster runs need extra force and the slope comes out high; the wheels’ rotational inertia, which adds to the effective mass, so the slope comes out high; sensor calibration, which scales every F reading. Note: “forgot to zero” or “track not level” shifts the intercept, not the slope, so it does not explain a wrong mass. Partial credit only. “Human error” earns nothing.
  5. Third law, Part C. The two readings were the same size at every moment (within about 0.01 N), held or moving. The GDX-FOR pulled the cart toward the pulley, and the cart pulled the GDX-FOR back toward the cart: equal size, opposite direction, acting on different objects.
  6. Percent difference. 2.1%, 3.8%, 1.0%, 2.0%; work above.
