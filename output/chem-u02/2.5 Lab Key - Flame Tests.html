FAILURE POINTS

  • Everything looks yellow. Sodium is everywhere, and the burning wood is yellow-orange. Say: "Record the first two seconds, then the stick goes in the sink."
  • Potassium's lilac is faint. Any trace of sodium swamps it. Students who see yellow at station 2 or for unknown A usually put a stick back in a beaker or touched the tip.
  • Sticks put back in a beaker. One used stick contaminates the beaker and every stick soaking in it, for every later group. Watch the first rotation closely; if it happens, swap the beaker and its sticks.
  • The stick catches fire. Usually from holding it in the flame too long. Tip out, into the sink, new stick.
  • Magnesium "looks orange." With no color of its own, students record the stick or a trace of sodium. Say: "If you saw nothing but the stick burning, that is the result."
  • "Red" for three compounds. Push for crimson, bright red or orange-red. Lithium, strontium and calcium are where students lose the unknown.

EXPECTED RESULTS

A good group records the reference color for all seven ions on the table and names all three unknowns. A group that records "red" without more detail still gets A and B, but cannot separate C from lithium chloride: that is exactly post-lab question 2.

PRE-LAB KEY

  1. Ground and excited state. The electron absorbs energy from the flame and jumps from its ground state to a higher level (excited state). It drops back and gives off the energy as a photon of light. Both terms must appear.
  2. Same color, NaNO3 and NaCl. The metal ion (sodium) makes the color, not the nitrate or chloride part.
  3. Hardest pair. Lithium (crimson red) and strontium (red); calcium (orange-red) is also accepted with one of them. An unknown red could be either, so color alone cannot decide.
  4. Magnesium, no color. The electrons are still excited and still give off photons when they drop, but the drops are too big: most of the light is ultraviolet, which our eyes cannot detect. Needs both halves, plus "ultraviolet." "Magnesium has no electrons that move" earns nothing.
  5. Part B, steps 1–2. A soaked stick from the station's beaker, held by the dry end; the wet tip goes at the edge of the flame, just above the blue inner cone.
  6. Part B, step 3. After two or three seconds the wood burns yellow-orange, which is the stick's color, not the solution's.
  7. Part B, steps 4–5. It goes in the sink. A stick put back into a beaker contaminates that solution and its soaking sticks for every later group.
  8. Stick on fire. Take it out of the flame and drop it in the sink; never on the bench or the trash. Full credit needs the eyewash location written correctly.
  9. Data table. Twelve rows in station order, three columns, in ink; expected colors filled from the reference table, magnesium from the Background, iron and the unknowns left blank.

POST-LAB KEY

  1. Identify A, B, C. A: potassium nitrate, lilac. B: copper(II) nitrate, blue-green. C: strontium chloride, red. Each needs the color from the student's own table. If a student names lithium chloride for C and backs it with a crimson-red observation, give partial credit and expect question 2 to catch it.
  2. Unknown with two matches. C (red) could be strontium chloride or lithium chloride. The two reds are too close to separate by eye, so the color is a clue, not proof. Accept another test or comparing C side by side with both known samples. Calcium nitrate is also accepted as a second match.
  3. Magnesium chloride. Little or no color beyond the stick. Its electrons are excited and drop back, but most of the photons are ultraviolet, shorter than about 400 nm, outside the visible range. Full credit names ultraviolet and says the drops still happen. Accept a faint color if the student says it was faint and still explains the ultraviolet.
  4. A mismatch, with a cause. Accept a real procedural cause tied to what they saw: recording after the wood started burning (yellow-orange), a stick put back in a beaker or a touched tip bringing in sodium (yellow over lilac), holding the tip too low or too high in the flame (faint color). For iron, describing the color they saw and saying it is not on the reference table is enough. "Human error" earns nothing.
