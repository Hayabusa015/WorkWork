PURPOSE

You will hold thirteen solutions in a flame and record the color each one gives. Ten are known compounds. The other three are unknowns, and the colors you record are the only evidence you will have to name them.

BACKGROUND

Heat excites electrons. In the flame, electrons in the metal ions absorb energy and jump from their ground state to higher energy levels. That excited state does not last. Each electron drops back down and gives off the energy as a photon of light.

The size of the drop sets the color. A photon's energy equals the gap between the two levels. A bigger drop gives more energy and a shorter wavelength, toward violet. A smaller drop gives a longer wavelength, toward red. Each metal has its own set of energy levels, so each gives off its own mix of colors.

The color comes from the metal ion. Sodium nitrate and sodium chloride both give a yellow flame, because the sodium ion is doing the work. That is why the table below lists ions, not whole compounds. Two cautions: the wooden stick itself burns yellow-orange once it catches, so watch the first two or three seconds; and a color is a clue, not proof, because two ions can give nearly the same color.

Not all light is visible. Your eyes only detect a narrow band of wavelengths, from violet (about 400 nm) to red (about 700 nm). A drop with a bigger energy gap than violet gives off ultraviolet light, shorter than 400 nm, and a smaller gap than red gives off infrared, longer than 700 nm. Both are real photons, and both are invisible. Magnesium is the example today: its electrons are excited and drop back like every other metal's, but most of the light it gives off is ultraviolet, so a magnesium compound adds little or no color you can see.

SAFETY

  • Splash goggles go on before any burner is lit and stay on until every station is cleaned up, including while other groups are still testing.
  • Long hair is tied back and loose sleeves are pushed up before you reach toward a flame. The flame on a burner can be nearly invisible in a bright room. Assume it is lit.
  • A stick left in the flame too long catches fire. Hold only the dry end, keep the tip in the flame for just a few seconds, and if the tip keeps burning, drop it in the sink. Never set a burning or used stick on the bench.
  • The nitrates on the list (sodium, potassium, calcium, barium, copper and iron nitrate) are oxidizers: they make wood and paper burn faster. Keep paper and notebooks away from the flame, and never hold a stick over or near the beakers.
  • Several solutions are harmful if swallowed or if they touch your skin or eyes, especially the barium and copper compounds; boric acid (station 3) may harm fertility or an unborn child. Hold only the dry end of a stick, and never taste, smell or touch a solution. If one gets on your skin, rinse it with running water and tell me. In your eyes: go straight to the eyewash, hold your eyelids open for at least 15 minutes, and send a group member for me immediately.
  • Do not pick up or move a beaker. If one tips over, step back and tell me. Do not wipe it up yourself.
  • Report every spill, including water. Water on the floor is how people fall.

PROCEDURE

Part A — Set up at your first station

  1. Go to the station I assign. Goggles on, hair tied back, sleeves up.
  2. Light the burner and adjust the air until the flame is steady and blue with a visible inner cone. A yellow, smoky flame needs more air.
  3. Read both labels. Find those two rows in your table before you test anything.

Part B — Test each solution ABOUT 5 MINUTES PER STATION

  1. Each student takes one soaked stick from the first beaker. Lift it straight out and hold it by the dry end. Do not stir or tap it against the beaker.
  2. Hold the wet tip at the edge of the flame, just above the blue inner cone.
  3. Watch the color the instant the tip enters the flame. Record it in the Actual color column before you do anything else.
    • After two or three seconds the wood itself starts to burn yellow-orange. That color is the stick, not the solution. Do not record it.
    • Each person tests and records their own sample. Compare colors with your group, but write what you saw.
  4. Drop the used stick in the sink. Never put a stick back into either beaker, even if it did not light: it carries soot and the other metal into the solution, and every group after you sees the wrong color.
  5. Repeat steps 1–4 with a stick from the second beaker. At station 3, repeat again with the third beaker, boric acid, and compare its green with barium's.
  6. Leave the burner lit. When I call "rotate," move to the next station number (station 6 moves to station 1). Do not move early.

Part C — The unknowns

  1. Unknowns A, B and C are tested at stations 5 and 6 exactly like the others. Each one is a compound you have already tested.
  2. Record the actual color in the unknown's row. Leave the identification for the post-lab.

DATA TABLES

You built Table 1 in your pre-lab. Have it open and ready before the first stick goes into the flame, and fill in each color as you see it, not at the end of the period.

Describe the color you see, not the color you expect. "Red" is not enough when there are two reds on the table: say crimson, bright red or orange-red, and whether it was strong or faint. If you write something wrong, draw one line through it and write the correction beside it.

DISPOSAL AND CLEANUP — LAST FIVE MINUTES

  1. Every used stick goes in the sink, never the trash can, where a smoldering stick could start a fire. I collect them from the sink after class.
  2. The beakers stay at their stations, exactly where they were. Do not pour any solution out, down the drain or anywhere else. I collect and store them.
  3. Turn the gas off at the valve, then leave the burner where it is until it is cool to touch. Leave any sticks still soaking where they are.
  4. Wash all glassware with the Alconox solution in the blue spray bottle. Spray inside, scrub with a brush, and rinse thoroughly — soap left in a beaker changes the next measurement made in it.
  5. Rinse with distilled water and invert everything on the rack to dry.
  6. Wipe your bench, push in your stool, and wash your hands. Goggles come off last, once the whole room is finished — not once you are.
