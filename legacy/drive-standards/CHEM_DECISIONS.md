## Course sequencing rules

- Begin the year with observations/inferences, lab equipment, an orientation, and
  foundational lab behavior.
- Atomic model/history is the first major academic-content arc.
- Delay the heavy math sequence rather than front-loading it — scientific notation,
  significant figures, conversions, and stoichiometry come later in the year.
- Significant figures are enforced consistently after midyear rather than becoming an early
  barrier.
- Durable skills (naming, formulas) may be revisited after their original unit.
- Conceptual understanding and data interpretation develop alongside calculations.

---

## Population and time structure

- Mixture of advanced sophomores and average juniors/seniors. 5–10 students may be poorly
  placed or need substantial prerequisite support.
- Lab groups usually 3–4 students, about six groups per class, rotate every ~3 weeks.
- Class period: 50 minutes. Reserve the final 5 minutes for cleanup/reset. Most labs fit one
  period including cleanup; post-lab analysis may finish outside class if needed.

---

## Section cycle

Lecture/direct instruction → guided and independent practice → lab or other application when
appropriate → post-lab analysis tied to the lesson. Not every section needs a lab.

---

## Practice progression

2–4 easy/foundational problems → 3–4 medium problems → 1–2 difficult/multi-step problems → 1
connection problem linking to earlier learning when appropriate. Builds confidence without
lowering final rigor.

---

## Assessment shape

### Quizzes (Friday)
5–6 questions, usually 3–4 equivalent versions, same skills and rigor with numbers/order
changed. Occasional essay quiz. May include 1–2 short prior-unit recall items, especially
durable skills like naming and formulas.

### Unit tests — two days
**Day 1, conceptual:** ~25 multiple choice, four versions (A–D), equivalent content and
rigor, strong distractors from real misconceptions.
**Day 2, computational:** ~5 free-response calculation problems, partial credit available,
work and units required. Covers the current unit only — never insert an earlier-unit
calculation just to make it cumulative.

Brief durable-skill recall may appear on quizzes and the conceptual test; the computational
test stays focused on the current unit. No curve. No retakes. Study guides align to concepts
and skills but never duplicate test questions.

---

## Grading and late-work policy

- Daily practice: 5-point rubric at the top of the page, emphasizing effort, work shown, and
  reasoning. ~10% of the overall course grade.
- Late work: −20% one day late, −40% total two days late, −50% max at three+ days late. Hard
  cutoff at the end of the unit.

---

## Reference packet contents

Given to students around Week 2, usable throughout the year including on tests:

- Periodic table
- Polyatomic ions, with an indication of which ones must be memorized
- Solubility rules
- Percent-error formula/reference

Do not test pure recall of anything in the packet unless the learning target explicitly
requires memorization.

## Laboratory design

### Student-facing template
Header (course/unit/section/lab code, Name/Date/Period, group line) → Topic/Goals → Purpose
→ Background (only the theory needed) → Safety (specific PPE and hazards, spill/exposure
response) → Pre-Lab (2–3 conceptual, 3–4 procedural-reading, one safety/disposal check) →
Procedure (numbered steps, bulleted substeps, exact quantities/times/temps/endpoints, formula
name + formula) → Data/Observations (table, precise qualitative description prompts, units in
headers) → Disposal/Cleanup (exact location/method, final 5 minutes preserved) → Post-Lab
Analysis (2–3 "what happened and why," calculations with work/units, percent error + accepted
value when applicable, error analysis that names a real procedural cause rather than "human
error").

### Teacher-facing template
Materials per group and per class · solution recipes and concentrations · advance-prep
timeline · room/equipment setup · expected duration by phase · safety/disposal notes ·
common failure points · expected data range and observations · worked calculations and
answers · cleanup/reset checklist · Missed Lab Analysis guidance.

### Missed lab
If the physical setup is already cleaned up, the student completes a Missed Lab Analysis
rather than the class re-running the lab.

### Lab data collection
One shared Google Sheet across all Chemistry periods per lab. Students enter group names and
data; the sheet auto-calculates class statistics (average, mean, median) and can reveal
prior-year data for comparison.

### Other lab rules
Students use McNeal carbonless lab notebooks, individually graded from the carbonless copy.
Goggles required for acids and whenever the hazard calls for it. Disposal instructions always
explicit, never implied.

---

## Data, graphing, and scientific reasoning

Use graphs, diagrams, tables, and models frequently. Ask students to interpret data, not only
calculate from it. Require correct axes, labels, units, scale. Ask about trend, relationship,
anomalies, uncertainty. Connect observations to particle-level or model-based explanations.
Prefer specific error reasoning over "human error."

---

## Differentiation

- **Modified `(M)`** — smaller steps, reteach prerequisites, preserve the core learning
  target.
- **Challenge** — more reasoning/transfer, not just more repetitive problems.
- **Substitute/case-study work** — fits one period, no new content, data/application-based.

---

## Demonstrations

5–8 minutes, near the start of a section, no written work unless requested, only when it
meaningfully supports the concept.

---

## Real-world connection themes

Health and engineering connections are particularly useful. Use realistic data and
situations — e.g. tie equilibrium and Le Châtelier's principle to biological, medical,
environmental, or engineering systems.

---

## Course material organization

One unit at a time, each a cohesive packet with sections inside it. Binder has a table of
contents with clear areas for quizzes, labs, homework/practice, and notes. Unit/section
references stay consistent across every associated assignment.

### Pacing principles
Build around actual instructional days, not generic weeks. Account for shortened weeks,
holidays, testing, likely disruption around breaks. Keep labs within one period. Preserve the
two-day unit-test structure. No new content on planned substitute days. Buffer time for hard
calculations, cleanup, reteaching, interruptions.

### Pacing guide — PROVISIONAL, needs Matt's validation once the year is underway

The four curriculum phases map onto the four quarters:

- Q1 (43 days / 9 wks) → Phase 1, Units 0–3 (Lab Ready)
- Q2 (41 days / 9 wks) → Phase 2, Units 4–7 (Bonding Logic)
- Q3 (43 days / 9 wks) → Phase 3, Units 8–11 (Reaction Math)
- Q4 (49 days / 10 wks) → Phase 4, Units 12–15 (Systems)

Within each quarter: roughly 1.5–2 days per section plus a 2-day Unit Test (Day 1 conceptual
MC, Day 2 computational FR) at the end of each unit, with a buffer/review week at the end of
each quarter or split around a major break. Short weeks get lighter content loads — no new
unit test lands on or right after one where avoidable. Significant-figure enforcement begins
Q3, matching the sequencing rule above.

A full week-by-week branded PDF pacing guide exists with exact section-by-week assignments,
lab/quiz/test flags, and a year-at-a-glance summary. Treat all day allocations as
PROVISIONAL until validated against real classroom pace.
