## 2. Course Identity and Instructional Philosophy — CONFIRMED

Matthew Shull teaches Chemistry, Physics, and Geology. Chemistry is the primary course and strongest personal interest. The course should feel like it belongs to Mr. Shull: professional, accurate, modern, organized, personal, and approachable without sounding like a textbook.

The goal is not to replace the course with a generic commercial curriculum. Matthew already has a built course and many existing files. The long-term goal is to reorganize, improve, standardize, and consistently brand those materials one unit at a time.

### Core philosophy

- Teach formal scientific definitions accurately, then explain them in clear teacher language.
- Target approximately a 10th–11th grade reading level.
- Build real conceptual understanding, not answer-pattern recognition.
- Require students to show reasoning and work.
- Use visual explanations, diagrams, graphs, tables, and data interpretation frequently.
- Use demonstrations only when they genuinely support the concept.
- Use paper-and-pencil materials extensively to reduce unproductive AI dependence.
- Keep the teacher's judgment central. AI may draft, organize, differentiate, and critique, but Matthew verifies the science, math, rigor, and classroom fit.

### Classroom population

- Approximately 40–50 Chemistry students across class periods.
- Mixture of advanced sophomores and average juniors/seniors.
- Typically 5–10 students may be poorly placed or need substantial prerequisite support.
- Student lab groups usually contain 3–4 students.
- Approximately six groups per class.
- Rotate groups about every three weeks.

### Time structure

- Class period: 50 minutes.
- Reserve the final 5 minutes for cleanup, teardown, and room reset when needed.
- Most labs should fit into one class period, including cleanup.
- If post-lab analysis cannot be finished during class, students may finish it outside class.

## 3. Reconstructed Chemistry Project Interview

### A. What should a normal section look like? — CONFIRMED

The default instructional cycle is:

1. Lecture/direct instruction.
2. Guided and independent practice.
3. Lab or other concept-supporting application when appropriate.
4. Post-lab analysis connected directly to the lesson.

Then the next section begins the cycle again. Not every section requires a lab.

### B. How should practice problems progress? — CONFIRMED

A typical lesson practice set should move through:

- 2–4 easy/foundational problems;
- 3–4 medium problems;
- 1–2 difficult or multi-step problems;
- 1 connection problem that links the new topic to earlier learning when appropriate.

This progression should build confidence without lowering the final level of rigor.

### C. What should notes look like? — CONFIRMED

- Move toward Cornell notes.
- Provide strong scaffolding at the beginning of the year.
- Gradually remove prompts and supports as students become more independent.
- Make the connection between the PowerPoint and Cornell notes obvious.
- Visually identify information students must write.
- Use concise bullets rather than dense paragraphs.
- Worked examples should be followed by a completed solution slide.

### D. What should PowerPoints look like? — CONFIRMED

- Colorful, visual, and purposeful.
- Bullet points rather than textbook-style paragraphs.
- Frequent diagrams, models, data tables, graphs, and relevant images.
- Section dividers should clearly show where students are in the unit.
- Must-write material should be highlighted in a distinct, consistent color.
- Each worked example should have a following slide with the complete solution.
- Visuals must teach or clarify; avoid decoration that adds no instructional value.
- Maintain the SHULL teacher brand throughout.

### E. How should daily student handouts look? — CONFIRMED

- Modern and professional.
- Printer-friendly and grayscale-safe.
- Space-efficient without feeling crowded.
- Trade Gothic Next is the primary font.
- Approximately 11-point body text for standard documents.
- Clear bold headings and predictable hierarchy.
- Standard Name / Date / Period fields.
- Assignment header should identify the unit and section, such as "Refer to Unit X, Section Y."
- Use a consistent compact document-code system.

### F. What should homework and practice answer support include? — CONFIRMED

- Print numerical answers in brackets for self-checking.
- Do not print answers for written-response, vocabulary, explanation, or graph-reading questions.
- Calculator use is allowed.
- Require units in dimensional-analysis work.
- Require students to show all steps.

### G. What should Friday quizzes look like? — CONFIRMED

- Usually 5–6 questions.
- Usually 3–4 equivalent versions.
- Versions should assess the same skills and rigor while changing numbers and/or order.
- Some Fridays may use an essay quiz instead.
- Quizzes may include 1–2 short prior-unit recall items when useful, especially durable skills such as naming and formulas.

### H. What should unit tests look like? — CONFIRMED

Unit tests are split across two days:

**Day 1: Conceptual test**

- Approximately 25 multiple-choice questions.
- Four versions: A, B, C, and D.
- Versions must be equivalent in content and rigor.
- Strong distractors should reflect real misconceptions.

**Day 2: Computational test**

- Approximately five free-response calculation problems.
- Partial credit is available.
- Students must show work and units.
- The computational test covers the current unit only; do not insert an earlier-unit calculation simply to make it cumulative.

**Cumulative distinction**

- Brief durable-skill recall may appear on quizzes and conceptual assessments.
- The separate computational free-response test should remain focused on the current unit.

Other test rules:

- An A-level student should demonstrate multi-step reasoning and durable knowledge, not merely memorize a review sheet.
- No curve.
- No retakes.
- Study guides align to concepts and skills but should not duplicate test questions.

### I. How should practice be graded? — CONFIRMED

- Daily practice uses a 5-point rubric placed at the top of the paper.
- The rubric emphasizes effort, work shown, and reasoning.
- Daily practice is approximately 10% of the overall course grade.

### J. What is the late-work policy? — CONFIRMED

- One day late: subtract 20%.
- Two days late: subtract an additional 20% (40% total).
- Three or more days late: subtract an additional 10% (50% total maximum late penalty).
- Hard cutoff: the end of the unit.

### K. How should labs be structured? — CONFIRMED

Each lab assignment should contain:

1. Topic / Goals.
2. Purpose.
3. Background.
4. Safety.
5. Pre-lab questions.
6. Procedures.
7. Disposal.
8. Post-lab analysis.

Lab details:

- Students use McNeal carbonless lab notebooks.
- Each student receives an individual grade using the carbonless copy.
- Pre-lab work normally includes 2–3 conceptual questions.
- Pre-lab work also includes 3–4 critical procedural questions.
- Include a question about the key safety or disposal issue.
- Procedural questions should force students to locate important values or actions, such as a target temperature.
- Procedures use numbered major steps with bullets for substeps.
- When a formula is used, include both the formula and the formula name.
- Safety directions name specific hazards: burns, fumes, corrosives, staining, etc.
- Goggles are required when working with acids and whenever dictated by the lab hazard.
- Disposal instructions must be explicit rather than implied.
- Observations should be descriptive and specific, such as exact color descriptions and whether bubbling was slight or vigorous.
- Post-lab analysis normally includes 2–3 questions about what happened and connects the results to the lesson.
- Percent error is always included when applicable.
- Provide the accepted value needed for percent error.
- Labs should be feasible in one 50-minute period with 5 minutes reserved for cleanup.

### L. What happens when a student misses a lab? — CONFIRMED

If the physical setup has already been cleaned up, the student completes a "Missed Lab Analysis" rather than requiring the class to recreate the entire lab.

### M. What teacher support should accompany a lab? — CONFIRMED

Each lab should include a teacher-facing section with:

- solution and material preparation;
- setup and timing guidance;
- likely student mistakes and practical pitfalls;
- expected observations and results;
- accepted or target values when appropriate;
- worked answers to the post-lab questions;
- cleanup and disposal reminders.

### N. How should lab data be collected? — CONFIRMED

- Use one Google Sheet for all Chemistry periods for each lab.
- Students enter group member names and group data.
- The sheet should automatically calculate useful class statistics such as averages, means, and medians.
- Include the option to reveal prior-year data for comparison.

### O. How should differentiation work? — CONFIRMED

**Modified version**

- Label with "(M)" in the header.
- Break complex work into smaller steps.
- Reteach missing prerequisite skills.
- Preserve the core scientific learning target.

**Challenge version**

- Provide a higher-rigor option when useful.
- Increase reasoning, transfer, or complexity rather than merely adding more repetitive problems.

**Substitute/case-study work**

- Case studies should usually fit one class period.
- Do not introduce brand-new content on substitute days.
- Use data analysis, application, and practice.

### P. What should demonstrations look like? — CONFIRMED

- Usually 5–8 minutes.
- Best placed near the beginning of a section.
- No written work required during the demo unless Matthew specifically requests it.
- Use only when the demonstration meaningfully supports the concept.

### Q. What real-world connections are preferred? — CONFIRMED

- Health and engineering connections are particularly useful.
- Use realistic data and situations.
- Example: connect equilibrium and Le Châtelier's principle to biological, medical, environmental, or engineering systems.

### R. How should course materials be organized? — CONFIRMED

- Build one unit at a time.
- Each unit is one cohesive packet with sections inside it.
- Notebook/binder organization includes a table of contents.
- Maintain clear areas for quizzes, labs, homework/practice, and notes.
- Use unit and section references consistently across every associated assignment.
- Standardize headers, footers, document codes, tone, and visual design across the entire course.

## 5. Course Sequencing Rules — CONFIRMED

- Begin the year with observations/inferences, lab equipment, a room/lab scavenger or orientation, and foundational lab behavior.
- Atomic model/history is the first major academic-content arc.
- Delay the heavy math sequence rather than front-loading it.
- Scientific notation, significant figures, conversions, and stoichiometry occur later in the year.
- Significant figures are enforced consistently after midyear rather than becoming an early barrier to Chemistry learning.
- Durable skills such as naming and formulas may be revisited after their original unit.
- Conceptual understanding and data interpretation should develop alongside calculations.

## 6. Reference Packet — CONFIRMED

Students receive a reference packet around Week 2 and may use it throughout the year, including during tests.

The packet includes at least:

- periodic table;
- polyatomic ions;
- indication of which key polyatomic ions must be memorized;
- solubility rules;
- percent-error formula/reference.

When creating assessments, do not test pure recall of information intentionally provided in the packet unless the learning target explicitly requires memorization.

## 9. Assessment Construction Rules — CONFIRMED

### General quality rules

- Match every item to an intended learning target.
- Solve and verify all calculations before finalizing the key.
- Check that all versions are genuinely equivalent.
- Avoid ambiguous wording and multiple defensible answers unless intentionally designed as open response.
- Distractors should represent plausible misconceptions.
- Require units wherever scientifically appropriate.
- Preserve partial-credit opportunities in free response.
- Include graphs, tables, diagrams, or experimental data when they improve the assessment.

### Study guides

- Cover the same concepts and skills as the test.
- Do not copy test questions.
- Include a balanced range of conceptual, representational, and computational preparation.

### Cumulative recall

- Use sparingly and purposefully.
- Best for durable knowledge such as naming, formulas, and foundational concepts.
- Do not turn current-unit computational tests into mixed earlier-unit calculation tests.

## 10. Laboratory Design System — CONFIRMED

### Student-facing lab template

**Header**

- Course / Unit / Section / Lab code.
- Student Name / Date / Period.
- Group-member line if needed.

**Topic / Goals**

- Concise learning goals written in student-friendly language.

**Purpose**

- What the student is investigating or determining.

**Background**

- Only the theory needed to understand and complete the lab.
- Define required formulas and scientific relationships.

**Safety**

- Specific PPE.
- Specific chemical, thermal, glassware, fume, stain, or disposal hazards.
- Required response to spills or exposure when relevant.

**Pre-Lab**

- 2–3 conceptual questions.
- 3–4 procedural-reading questions.
- At least one safety/disposal check.

**Procedure**

- Numbered major steps.
- Bulleted substeps.
- Exact quantities, times, temperatures, and endpoints.
- Formula plus formula name where used.

**Data / Observations**

- Organized table whenever possible.
- Prompt for precise qualitative descriptions.
- Include units in column headers.

**Disposal / Cleanup**

- Exact disposal location/method.
- Equipment washing, bench cleaning, and return expectations.
- Preserve the final five minutes.

**Post-Lab Analysis**

- 2–3 "what happened and why?" questions.
- Direct connection to section learning.
- Calculations with work and units.
- Percent error plus supplied accepted value when applicable.
- Error analysis that distinguishes procedural error from vague "human error."

### Teacher-facing lab template

- Materials per group and total materials per class.
- Solution recipes and concentrations.
- Advance preparation timeline.
- Room/equipment setup.
- Expected duration by phase.
- Safety and disposal notes.
- Common failure points.
- Expected data range and observations.
- Worked calculations and answers.
- Cleanup/reset checklist.
- Missed Lab Analysis guidance.

## 11. Data, Graphing, and Scientific Reasoning — CONFIRMED

- Use graphs, diagrams, tables, and models frequently.
- Ask students to interpret data, not only calculate from it.
- Require correct axes, labels, units, and scale when graphing.
- Include questions about trend, relationship, anomalies, and uncertainty.
- Connect experimental observations to particle-level or model-based explanations.
- Prefer specific error reasoning over "human error."
- Use one shared lab-data sheet across Chemistry periods when class comparison adds value.
