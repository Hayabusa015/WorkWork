## 2. Course Identity and Instructional Philosophy — CONFIRMED

Matthew Shull teaches Chemistry, Physics, and Geology. Chemistry is his primary course and strongest personal interest, built for a mixed advanced/average academic population. **Geology is a distinctly different course and should not be treated as "Chemistry with rocks."**

### Who this course is for

- Geology primarily serves students who need a science credit to graduate — not the advanced/AP-adjacent population found in Chemistry.
- There is intentionally **less rigor** than Chemistry. Do not import Chemistry's computational depth, multi-step reasoning load, or dense content pacing into Geology material.
- Matthew cannot lecture as heavily with PowerPoints in this class — the curriculum needs to be built *around* the students, not delivered at them.

### Core instructional philosophy

- Favor **visuals and diagrams over text-heavy content.** Every concept should have a strong visual anchor (diagram, labeled image, map, model, timeline).
- **Cut, color, and glue projects are a first-class instructional tool in this course**, not filler — use foldables, interactive notebook pages, cut-and-sequence activities, and diagram-labeling/coloring tasks generously to keep students engaged.
- **Graphic organizers** (flowcharts, comparison charts, fill-in diagrams, sequencing charts, cause/effect maps) should be a default format for content delivery and note-taking, more so than in Chemistry.
- Keep reading level and vocabulary load lower than Chemistry. Prioritize comprehension and engagement over dense scientific writing.
- Teach real vocabulary and real science — this is not a "watered down to meaninglessness" course — but scaffold it heavily and don't assume strong independent reading or math skills.
- Keep the teacher's judgment central. AI may draft, organize, and differentiate, but Matthew verifies science accuracy, classroom fit, and rigor level.

### Classroom population — CONFIRMED

- Similar scale to Chemistry: roughly 40–50 Geology students across class periods.
- Skews toward lower-level/credit-recovery-type students rather than Chemistry's advanced-to-average mix. Build the curriculum to fit this population rather than assuming Chemistry's baseline.

### Time structure — CARRIED OVER (unconfirmed)

- Class period: 50 minutes (same district/schedule as Chemistry).
- Reserve time at the end of the period for cleanup/reset when an activity requires it.
- Most activities should fit within one class period.

## 3. Activities, Not Formal Labs — CONFIRMED

This is one of the biggest differences from Chemistry. Geology does **not** run a formal pre-lab/procedure/post-lab wet-lab program the way Chemistry does.

- The only real hands-on "labs" in this course involve **rocks and minerals** (physical specimen identification, mineral property testing, rock-type stations).
- Everything else is built as an **activity** — simulations, diagram work, modeling, cut/color/glue projects, and graphic organizers — rather than a formal lab report cycle.
- Matthew actively seeks out **online simulations** for students to work through where good ones exist (e.g., plate boundary simulators, seismic wave/earthquake locating simulations, glacier movement models, rock cycle interactives).
- **Do not default to or recommend Gizmos.** Matthew has access to a Gizmos subscription through the school but avoids it in this class — students get little out of it and tend to cheat through it. Prefer purpose-built simulations, hands-on stations, or original activities instead.
- When a rock/mineral lab is built, it can follow a lighter version of the lab template (goal, brief background, procedure/ID steps, data table, a few analysis questions) — it does not need Chemistry's full pre-lab/safety/disposal apparatus unless a real hazard is present.

## 4. Assessment Structure — CONFIRMED

Geology assessments are simpler and lower-stakes than Chemistry's two-day conceptual/computational test structure.

- **Single-day tests and quizzes** — no separate computational test day.
- Question types lean heavily on **multiple choice and matching**, with labeled-diagram and fill-in-the-blank items as appropriate.
- Avoid multi-step calculation-heavy free response items; this course is not computation-driven the way Chemistry is.
- Completed graphic organizers, foldables, and cut/color/glue projects are legitimate, valued components of how understanding is demonstrated in this course — not just "extra" engagement pieces layered on top of a traditional test.
- **CARRIED OVER (unconfirmed) from Chemistry, pending Matthew's confirmation for Geology:**
  - Daily practice graded on a rubric (Chemistry uses a 5-point effort/work-shown rubric at ~10% of course grade).
  - Late-work penalty scale (Chemistry: −20% day 1, −40% total day 2, −50% max day 3+, hard cutoff at end of unit).
  - No curve, no retakes.
  - Treat these as reasonable defaults to keep consistency across Matthew's courses, but flag them as unconfirmed for Geology specifically until he confirms or adjusts.

## 5. Curriculum Map — CONFIRMED (Units 1–10, Matthew's stated sequence)

This is Matthew's actual teaching sequence, as he described it. Units 8, 9, and 10 below have been outlined against Ohio's Physical Geology (9–12) content standards per his request; treat those specific bullet expansions as **PROVISIONAL** until he reviews them. Unit 7 has been expanded with suggested additions he asked for — also **PROVISIONAL**.

### Unit 1: The Universe & Solar System

- The Big Bang
- Formation of the universe / early stars
- The scale of the universe
- Formation of a solar system
- Our star (structure/layers, star cycles, solar wind, etc.)
- Birth of the Earth

### Unit 2: Geologic Time — CONFIRMED, distinctive structure

- Geologic Time Scale overview
- Eons, Eras, Epochs, and Periods
- **Period-by-period deep dive:** roughly one period per day/section, each covering that period's geology, what Earth looked like, dominant life, and notable organisms (e.g., the Cambrian section covers the Cambrian Explosion, Earth's geology and appearance at the time, and dominant/notable lifeforms).
- Supercontinents and their movement through time.
- Mass extinction events — cause and result of each.
- **Running student project:** students add to a personal geologic timeline throughout the unit, section by section.

### Unit 3: Rocks & Minerals

- Minerals — full property/classification rules, ID criteria → **Mineral Lab**
- Rocks — igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic types in depth, rock cycle → **daily rock-type lab/station**, one rock type explored per day

### Unit 4: Plate Tectonics

- Layers of the Earth
- Continental drift
- Paleomagnetism
- Boundary types with examples, including the different convergent boundary subtypes (continental–continental, oceanic–continental, oceanic–oceanic), divergent, and transform
- Hot spots

### Unit 5: Volcanoes

- Magma types and composition
- Volcano types and composition
- Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI)
- Types of volcanoes (shield, composite/stratovolcano, cinder cone, etc.)
- Volcanic hazards

### Unit 6: Earthquakes

- Elastic rebound theory
- Fault types
- Earthquake properties (focus, epicenter, magnitude vs. intensity)
- Types of seismic waves (P, S, surface)
- Tsunamis

### Unit 7: Caves — PROVISIONAL additions beyond Matthew's original outline

Matthew's original outline: Karst topography breakdown (chemical dissolution process), types of caves. Suggested additions to round the unit out (confirm/cut as desired):

- **Speleothems** — stalactites, stalagmites, columns, flowstone, and the chemistry of how they form (ties directly back to the karst/dissolution chemistry already planned).
- **Cave types beyond solution caves** — briefly cover lava tube caves, sea caves, and glacier caves for contrast, while keeping solution/karst caves as the main focus.
- **Cave ecosystems** — troglobites/troglophytes, bats, and how food webs work in a no-light environment.
- **Ohio cave connections** — Ohio Caverns, Seneca Caverns, and Zane Caverns are real, visitable Ohio show caves; strong local-relevance material and potential virtual-tour resources.
- **Cave safety and conservation** — white-nose syndrome and bat population impacts, safe caving/spelunking practices, and why cave formations shouldn't be touched or broken.

### Unit 8: Glacial Geology — PROVISIONAL, built per Ohio Physical Geology standards (PG.GG)

Matthew asked for this to be outlined per Ohio standards. Ohio's Physical Geology standards (topic PG.GG — Glacial Geology) emphasize *process and evidence* over memorizing glacier-type names, and specifically call out tracing Ohio's own glacial history using real data.

- Glacier formation and the two broad types (continental ice sheets vs. alpine/valley glaciers) — keep this brief; the standard explicitly says the focus should be process, not memorizing glacier-type names.
- Glacial movement — accumulation zone, ablation zone, basal sliding.
- Erosional glacial features — cirques, arêtes, horns, U-shaped valleys, fjords, striations.
- Depositional glacial features — moraines (terminal/lateral/ground), till, outwash plains, erratics, kettles, eskers, drumlins.
- **Ohio's own glacial history** — the Wisconsinan and Illinoian ice sheets, Ohio's glacial boundary line, and how Ohio's landscape (including the Great Lakes) was shaped by glaciation. Strong local-relevance opportunity — a state glacial boundary map makes an excellent visual/coloring activity.
- Ice cores as climate evidence — sets up the climate-history content in Unit 9.
- Present-day glaciers and monitoring — modern retreat/advance data, sea-level connection.

### Unit 9: Oceans & Climate — PROVISIONAL, built per Ohio Physical Geology standards

Ohio's standards don't have oceans/climate as their own separate strand — ocean features live inside the Igneous/Metamorphic/Sedimentary topic (PG.IMS.CE.5), and climate evidence is tied into Glacial Geology (PG.GG.CE.1) via ice cores. Combining them into one unit, as Matthew's outline does, is a reasonable and standards-consistent structure.

- Ocean floor features — continental shelf, slope, and rise; abyssal plain; ocean trenches; mid-ocean ridges (ties back to Unit 4 plate tectonics).
- Ocean water properties — salinity, temperature, density, and ocean layering.
- Ocean currents — wind-driven surface currents and the Coriolis effect; deep thermohaline circulation.
- Tides and waves.
- Sea-level change — evidence, causes, past and present.
- Earth's climate system basics — atmosphere–ocean interaction, greenhouse effect.
- Climate evidence — ice cores and sediment cores (direct callback to Unit 8).
- Present climate trends — keep this data-interpretation-focused (reading graphs/data, not a policy debate).

### Unit 10: Earth's Resources — PROVISIONAL, added per Matthew's request, built per Ohio Physical Geology standards (PG.ER)

This unit was missing from Matthew's original 9-unit outline — it's the one topic strand in Ohio's official Physical Geology standards (PG.ER) that wasn't otherwise covered. Matthew asked for it to be added back in wherever it fits best in the course flow.

**Placement rationale:** placed as the final unit, after Oceans & Climate, rather than earlier in the sequence. Earth's Resources works best as a capstone because it pulls together threads from across the whole year — rocks/minerals become ores and fossil fuels (Unit 3), groundwater ties back to karst/caves (Unit 7), soil formation ties back to weathering and glacial deposits (Unit 8), and the conservation/climate framing closes the loop with Unit 9. It also gives the course a real-world, current-events-driven finish, which fits this class's engagement style better as a send-off than as a mid-year detour.

Suggested section breakdown:

- **Renewable vs. Nonrenewable Resources** — define and classify: solar, wind, hydro, geothermal vs. coal, oil, natural gas, and mined ores.
- **Fossil Fuels: Formation & Extraction** — how coal, oil, and natural gas form from sediment and rock over geologic time (direct callback to Unit 3's rock cycle and Unit 2's geologic time scale); basic extraction methods and environmental cost.
- **Ohio's Energy Resources** — coal, natural gas/fracking, and wind power in Ohio specifically, using real local data and maps; strong candidate for a map-based graphic organizer or coloring activity given the brand's local-relevance emphasis.
- **Water Resources** — groundwater and aquifers (callback to Unit 7 karst/cave systems), surface water, and water use/conservation.
- **Soil Resources** — soil formation through weathering (callback to rock weathering and Unit 8 glacial deposits), soil conservation, and erosion.
- **Air Resources & Pollution** — basic air quality concepts, tied loosely to the climate content from Unit 9.
- **Resource Conservation & Trade-offs** — feasibility, availability, and environmental cost of resource use and conservation decisions at a local-to-global scale; good fit for a debate-style graphic organizer or decision-mapping activity rather than a lecture.

## 7. SHULL Teacher Brand — CONFIRMED (same brand, Geology-weighted icon set)

Matthew confirmed the brand carries over from Chemistry unchanged — same fonts, same palette, same watermark system. Only the icon emphasis shifts toward Geology subject matter.

### Typography

- Primary font family: **Trade Gothic Next**.
- Fallback stack: Oswald (display) / Inter or Source Sans 3 (digital/document) when Trade Gothic Next is unavailable; secondary fallback Poppins (display) / Lato (body).
- Given this course's lower reading level and heavier visual load, err toward slightly larger body text and shorter text blocks than Chemistry uses, while keeping the same typeface system.

### Teacher color palette (identical to Chemistry)

| Token | Hex | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Deep Forest | `#1A2318` | Primary dark background/brand source |
| Slate Stone | `#2C3B2D` | Secondary dark neutral |
| Moss Green | `#4A7C59` | Primary accent |
| Warm Earth | `#8B6914` | Secondary accent/divider |
| Bio Lime | `#A8C97F` | Highlight/must-write cue |
| Parchment | `#EDF0E5` | Light text/background |

### Visual rules

- All combinations must remain readable in grayscale (most Geology handouts will print in grayscale, so this matters even more here than in Chemistry).
- Use only one accent-color word per headline; no text gradients.
- Use the Atom Seal watermark at 8–15% opacity when appropriate, consistent with Chemistry.
- Standard fields: Name / Date / Period.
- **Favor Geology-specific icons over Chemistry ones for this course:** geology hammer, magnifying glass, mountain/volcano, rock layers, wave, compass, timeline/clock, map. Reserve the atom/beaker/molecule icon set for Chemistry materials.
- For cut/color/glue projects and foldables, design with clear fold lines, generous cut margins, and print-safe (non-bleeding) color regions — these need to survive scissors and glue sticks in a 50-minute period.
- For dense tabular material (data sheets, comparison charts), prefer real tables over card/flexbox layouts.

### Geology-specific asset types (in addition to Chemistry's reusable library)

- foldables and interactive-notebook pages;
- cut-and-sequence activities (e.g., sequencing geologic periods, rock cycle steps, wave types);
- diagram-labeling and coloring pages;
- graphic organizers (flowcharts, comparison charts, cause/effect maps) as a primary note-taking format, not a supplement to lecture;
- timeline project pages (for the Unit 2 running geologic timeline);
- map-based activities (Ohio glacial boundary map, plate boundary world map, ocean floor map, Ohio energy-resource map for Unit 10).
