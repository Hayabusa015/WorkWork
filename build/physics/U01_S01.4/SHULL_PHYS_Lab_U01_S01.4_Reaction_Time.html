GOALS

  • Measure the distance a falling meter stick travels before you can catch it, to the nearest 0.1 cm.
  • Calculate a reaction time from that distance using the kinematic equation for free fall.
  • Compare your reaction time across three attention conditions and find which costs you most.
  • Determine how far a car travels while its driver is still deciding to brake.
  • Record every drop as you take it — including the ones you throw out, and why.

PURPOSE

You are going to measure your own reaction time with nothing but a meter stick and the fact that gravity is dependable — then do it twice more, once with your eyes closed and once with your eyes open and your brain busy. The last number you calculate today is a stopping distance, and it is the one that matters.

BACKGROUND

Free fall does not just mean "falling." An object is in free fall when gravity is the only force acting on it. A dropped meter stick qualifies — air resistance on something that dense, that slow, over that short a drop is far too small to detect with a ruler. A sheet of paper does not. The misstep nearly everyone makes is assuming the heavier object falls faster. It does not. Mass does not appear anywhere in the equations you are about to use, which says it plainly.

Gravity adds 9.80 m/s of speed every second. Near Earth's surface the acceleration due to gravity has a magnitude of g = 9.80 m/s2. That is an acceleration — a rate of change of velocity — not a speed and not a force. After 0.20 s of falling the stick is moving about 2.0 m/s; after 0.40 s, about 3.9 m/s. It never stops speeding up the whole way down.

Sign convention for this lab: down is negative. Declare it once and use it in every line of every calculation. Then the acceleration is a = −9.80 m/s2, and a stick that falls 18.4 cm has a displacement of Δy = −0.184 m. Both are negative, they divide to a positive number, and the time comes out positive — which it must, because clocks do not run backwards. If you end up taking the square root of a negative number, you mixed your signs. You did not break physics.

DISPLACEMENT WITH CONSTANT ACCELERATION
Δy = v0t + ½at2  ·  meters, meters per second, seconds

The stick starts at rest, and that is what makes this simple. At the instant Student A lets go the stick is not moving, so v0 = 0, the entire first term is zero and disappears, and rearranging what is left for time gives you the only equation you need today.

FREE-FALL TIME FROM DROP DISTANCE  (valid only when v0 = 0)
t = √( 2Δy ÷ a )  ·  seconds

The meter stick is a clock. It is not testing your grip or your arm speed — it turns a time you have no instrument to measure into a distance you can. Everything between the stick leaving Student A's fingers and your fingers closing — light reaching your eye, your brain recognizing it, the signal going down your arm — happens in free fall. The further it falls, the longer you took.

PERCENT ERROR
percent error = ( | experimental − accepted | ÷ accepted ) × 100

Accepted value for Situation A: 0.20 s. Published simple visual reaction times for adults run roughly 0.18–0.25 s. Know what that is before you use it: a typical value for people, not a constant of nature like g. A large percent error here means you are unusual, not that you measured badly — and your error analysis has to say which one you think it is.

SAMPLE CALCULATION — WORK YOURS EXACTLY LIKE THIS

A group averages their four drops and gets 18.4 cm. Find the reaction time, then the percent error.

  1. Convert to meters, put the sign on it, and list what you know. The equation runs in meters, not centimeters, and the stick fell down.
    18.4 cm × ( 1 m ÷ 100 cm ) = 0.184 m  →  Δy = −0.184 m
    Δy = −0.184 m  ·  v0 = 0 m/s  ·  a = −9.80 m/s2  ·  t = ?
  2. Start from the full equation and cancel the term you know is zero.
    Δy = v0t + ½at2  →  Δy = ½at2
  3. Solve for t before you put numbers in.
    t2 = 2Δy ÷ a  →  t = √( 2Δy ÷ a )
  4. Substitute, and watch the units cancel. Meters over meters per second squared leaves seconds squared; its square root is seconds.
    t = √( 2(−0.184 m) ÷ (−9.80 m/s2) ) = √( 0.03755 s2 )
  5. Answer, with a unit and the right significant figures. 18.4 cm has three, so t has three.
    t = 0.194 s
  6. Percent error against the accepted 0.20 s.
    ( | 0.194 s − 0.20 s | ÷ 0.20 s ) × 100 = 3.0%
Look at the square root before you go further. A stick falling 18.4 cm takes 0.194 s; one falling four times as far takes only twice as long. Reaction time does not scale with distance the way your eye wants it to, because the stick speeds up the whole way down.

U01 / S01.4 · REACTION TIME · PROCEDURE

MATERIALS

Per group of three: meter stick (1) · desk or table with a flat front edge · calculator (1) · lab notebook and pen, one per person

PROCEDURE

Roles. A is the dropper, B is the catcher — the one being measured — and C is the caller and recorder. Everyone is the catcher for four drops in every situation, so you rotate three times per part.

Part A — Situation A: Ordinary Reaction Time

  1. B sits with a forearm flat on the desk and the hand hanging past the front edge, thumb and index finger open about 3 cm apart.
    • The fingers must not touch the stick. If they do, B feels the release instead of seeing it and the number is worthless.
  2. A holds the meter stick vertically and hanging, so the 0 cm end is at the bottom and level with the top of B's thumb.
    • If your stick reads 100 at the bottom, turn it over — otherwise every number you record is wrong by the length of the stick.
  3. A says "ready," then releases at a random moment within five seconds. No countdown, no "three, two, one," no twitch of the arm first.
  4. B squeezes the instant the stick moves — close the fingers where they already are, do not chase the stick down.
  5. C reads the mark level with the top of B's thumb, to the nearest 0.1 cm. That number is the distance the stick fell.
  6. Repeat until B has four good drops. A drop is void if B chased the stick down, if B guessed the release and caught early, or if the stick went past B's hand entirely.
    • Void it, write down why, and take a replacement drop. Do not quietly keep a bad number and do not quietly delete one.
  7. Rotate until all three of you have four good drops.

Part B — Situation B: The Passenger's Warning EYES CLOSED

Same setup as Part A, with two changes: B's eyes are shut, and C now triggers the catch.

  1. B closes both eyes and keeps them closed until the catch, hand in the Part A position.
  2. C stands where the stick is clearly visible and watches the stick itself, not A's hand or face.
  3. A releases with no warning. C shouts "GO!" the instant the stick starts to move.
  4. B squeezes on the word — not on a guess, and not on the sound of A's hand opening.
  5. After the catch, C reads the mark at the top of B's thumb, to 0.1 cm.
    • If the stick falls all the way past B's hand, that is real data. Record it as "missed — greater than 100.0 cm." It means the warning arrived later than a full meter of falling.
  6. Four good drops each, then rotate.

Part C — Situation C: Eyes Open, Mind Busy KEEP COUNTING

Setup is identical to Part A, and B's eyes stay open and on the stick the entire time — the only thing that changes is what B's brain is doing. Four good drops each, then rotate, exactly as before.

  1. C gives B a starting number — begin at 100 — and B counts backwards out loud by sevens: 100, 93, 86, 79, and so on.
  2. B keeps counting out loud and watching the stick — both at once. That is the point.
  3. A releases at a random moment, after B has said at least three numbers.
  4. B catches, and keeps counting through the catch. If B stops counting in order to catch, the trial is void. Take it again.
  5. C records to 0.1 cm, using a different starting number each round (100, 96, 87, 94...) so nobody memorizes the sequence.

    The push. Everything before your foot touches the brake happens at a constant 25 mph, because you have not braked yet. That is exactly why v = d ÷ t works for the reaction distance and the kinematic equations do not — there is no acceleration during your reaction time. The braking distance is the same 8.9 m either way, because once the brakes are on the car does not care why it took you so long to get there. The entire difference between your two totals is distance you covered while you were still deciding.
