FAILURE POINTS

  • The stick is upside down. 100 cm at the bottom instead of 0. Every distance is then wrong by the length of the stick and the reaction times bunch up around 0.38–0.43 s (a real 11–31 cm reads as 89–69 cm). Nobody reacts that uniformly, and that tell is the giveaway. Catch it in the first minute by walking the room — it is invisible in the data afterward.
  • Fingers already touching the stick. The catcher feels the release instead of seeing it and posts times near 0.10 s. Enforce the 3 cm gap.
  • Anticipation. Short outlier drops — under about 8 cm — are almost always a guessed release. Tell the dropper to vary the delay and tell the catcher to watch the 0 cm end of the stick, not the dropper's hand or face.
  • A Situation B miss gets re-dropped until they "get one." This is the most damaging failure in the lab, because it deletes the result. A stick that falls past the hand means t > 0.452 s, and that is the finding, not a failed trial. Say this before Part B starts.
  • cm left as m. 18.4 entered as meters gives t = 1.94 s. Any reaction time over about 0.452 s that is not a recorded Situation B miss is a unit error — 0.452 s is the ceiling a 100.0 cm stick imposes, so a caught stick cannot beat it. That single sentence catches most of the bad post-labs.
  • Square root of a negative. Means they made Δy negative and a positive, or the reverse. Send them back to the sign convention they wrote in pre-lab Q4.
  • Counting backwards by sevens is genuinely hard for some students and for anyone working in a second language. Counting back by threes, or reciting the alphabet backwards, loads attention the same way. Swap it without comment — the load is the variable, not the arithmetic.

Grade the method, not the number. Every student's value is legitimately their own, and the only hard bound is the meter stick itself — a caught stick cannot exceed 100.0 cm, so no measured reaction time can exceed 0.452 s.

PRE-LAB KEY

  1. Free fall definition. Gravity is the only force acting. Ignoring air resistance. Accept any answer naming the stick's density, its low speed, and the short drop distance as the reason drag is negligible. Do not accept "because we said so" or "air doesn't matter."
  2. 50 kg vs 90 kg student. The sticks land at the same time. The justification must reach acceleration: every object in free fall accelerates at 9.80 m/s2 regardless of mass, and the mass of the person dropping it is irrelevant in the first place. Half credit for the right answer with a weight-based justification.
  3. Why it measures reaction time. The stick is in free fall for exactly the interval between release and the fingers closing. The distance is a stand-in for that time. Grip strength changes how hard they squeeze, not when.
  4. Sign convention. Down negative: a = −9.80 m/s2, Δy negative. Up-positive is the convention; accept down-positive if they then write a = +9.80 and Δy positive and use it consistently. The requirement is that it is declared, not which one they pick.
  5. Part A measurement. Distance the stick fell · meter stick · to 0.1 cm. All three required.
  6. Three void conditions. Chased the stick down · guessed the release and caught early · stick went past the hand. First two make the average look faster than reality; the third, if recorded as a short catch rather than a miss, also makes it look faster. Accept "slower" only for a genuinely recorded miss.
  7. Student C's Part B job. Calling "GO!" the instant the stick is released — and watching the stick itself, not A's hand or face. The second half is the point of the question: a caller who watches A's hand is the most common way Part B data goes bad, and this is the question that tells you whether they read the procedure.
  8. Three tables drawn. Full credit = three tables, in ink, units in the column headings, room to write. No credit for one table with the situations crammed in.

WORKING YOUR DATA — KEY

  1. Averages. Four distances per person per situation, cm then m. Watch for groups averaging across people instead of across drops — that produces one number per situation and destroys the within-person comparison Q1 depends on.
  2. Reaction times. t = √(2Δy ÷ a), three sig figs, signs carried. Cross-check against the table in EXPECTED RESULTS above.
  3. Percent error, Situation A only. ( | tA − 0.20 | ÷ 0.20 ) × 100. Worked example: t = 0.194 s → ( 0.006 ÷ 0.20 ) × 100 = 3%. One sig fig — the subtraction leaves 0.006, which has one. A student reporting 3.0% has claimed a precision the subtraction does not support; take it as a sig-fig error, not a wrong answer.
  4. Spread. Highest minus lowest drop, Situation A, in cm. Typically 1–4 cm. This is the uncertainty on their measured value. No post-lab question uses it — it is your diagnostic. Check it against the 1–4 cm benchmark in EXPECTED RESULTS before deciding whether an odd reaction time is real or procedural. A tight spread means the procedure was repeatable, not that the value is correct — an upside-down stick has a tiny spread too.
  5. Reset. Not graded. Checked.

POST-LAB KEY

Worked below with one illustrative student: A = 0.19 s · B = 0.42 s · C = 0.30 s. Every student's numbers differ. Mark the method.

  1. Rank and compare. Expected ranking fastest to slowest: A, C, B. Difference C − A = 0.30 − 0.19 = 0.11 s. Percent increase = ( 0.11 ÷ 0.19 ) × 100 = 58%. The sentence must land on attention, not vision — eyes were open and on the stick in both. Accept "my brain was busy," "I was doing two things," "I was processing the arithmetic." Do not accept "I couldn't see it."
  2. The school zone.
    1. 25 mi/h × (1609 m ÷ 1 mi) × (1 h ÷ 3600 s) = 11.2 m/s (11.17 unrounded).
    2. d = vt = 11.2 × 0.19 = 2.1 m
    3. d = vt = 11.2 × 0.30 = 3.4 m
    4. Attentive: 2.1 + 8.9 = 11.0 m. Distracted: 3.4 + 8.9 = 12.3 m.
    5. 1.2–1.3 m, and both are right. Straight from the rounded totals, 12.3 − 11.0 = 1.3 m. Carried unrounded, 11.2 × (0.30 − 0.19) = 1.23 m → 1.2 m. Accept either — but a student whose difference disagrees with their own parts (b)–(d) has an arithmetic error, not a rounding one. That is roughly 40% of the 3.0 m crosswalk; the child standing in the middle is 1.5 m from the near edge, so the extra distance is close to the whole margin between stopping at the edge of the crosswalk and arriving where the child is standing. Accept any one-sentence version that connects the number to the child. Do not accept a paragraph, and do not accept "you should pay attention" with no number in it.
The move students miss in Q2 is that v = d ÷ t is legal for the reaction distance precisely because nothing is accelerating yet — the car is still at a constant 25 mph until the brake is touched. Students who reach for a kinematic equation there have not understood which phase is which. The handout's "push" box says this outright; if a student still misses it, the question to ask is "what is the acceleration during your reaction time?"
