# Voice

**Authority:** How SHULL materials are written. `standards/ANTI_AI_SLOP_STANDARD.md` governs whether
a thing should exist; this governs how it sounds.
**Course adjustments** live in `courses/<course>/DECISIONS.md`, not here.

---

## The voice

A knowledgeable teacher talking to his own 10th–12th graders. Direct, plain, specific about what to
show and turn in. Says "you" and "your group." Confident without being stiff. Concise in directions,
more explanatory where students need the why.

Reading level roughly 10th–11th grade. Contractions are fine where natural.

### It should be

Clear for grades 10–12 · confident, not corporate · concise in directions · more explanatory where
students need help understanding why · willing to say "you," "we," "your group" · specific about
what students must show, calculate, observe, discuss, or submit · slightly informal where a real
teacher would be.

### It should not be

Relentlessly cheerful · motivational-speaker language · a textbook imitation · a customer-service
voice · full of education jargon · excessively polished, symmetrical, or repetitive · written as
though every routine assignment is an epic adventure.

---

## Sounds like

> "Show enough work that I can follow your reasoning."
> "Include units throughout the calculation."
> "If your answer seems unreasonable, go back and check the setup."
> "Use the evidence from the data table — not a guess."
> "You do not need a complete sentence for this one."
> "Before you begin, make sure your group agrees on…"
> "Be specific. 'It changed' is not an observation."

## Never

"Dive into…" · "Embark on a journey…" · "Unlock your potential…" · "In today's fast-paced world…" ·
"This engaging activity will…" · "Let's explore the fascinating world of…" · "It is important to
note that…" · "By the end of this activity, students will be able to…" unless a formal objective was
actually requested · "Remember, there are no wrong answers!" when answers can be wrong · "Great
job!" or "You've got this!" as filler · "critical thinking," "real-world application,"
"collaboration," or "inquiry" unless the material demonstrates exactly what those mean.

Do not overuse em dashes, semicolons, rhetorical questions, parenthetical asides, or three-part
slogan sentences.

---

## Directions

Tell students exactly what to do and what counts as complete.

- Keep routine directions short.
- Numbered steps only when order matters. Bullets for materials, conditions, choices, substeps.
- State calculator, reference-sheet, unit, rounding, and work-showing expectations where relevant.
- **Put a warning beside the step where students need it**, not in a block at the top.
- Distinguish individual work from group work.
- Say what must be turned in.
- Remove instructions students already know, unless the reminder prevents a common mistake.

> **No opening paragraph explaining the educational value of the activity.** Students need the task,
> not a sales pitch.

---

## Two rules that are not stylistic

- **"Human error" is never an acceptable error analysis.** Require a specific procedural cause.
- **Never invent a statement, event, opinion, or classroom story and attribute it to Mr. Shull.**

---

## Reading the draft

Read the directions aloud in your head. **Any sentence that sounds like a brochure gets rewritten.**
That is QA gate step 6, and it catches more than any list.
